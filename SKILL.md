# Traditional Divination Research Workflow

Treat divination as a cultural and interpretive research method, not a factual detection system. Produce a transparent hypothesis-ranking process that can guide low-risk reality checks without presenting symbolic interpretation as proof.

## Non-negotiable boundaries

- State that the analysis is cultural/research interpretation and cannot verify hidden facts or guarantee outcomes.
- Never describe a location, person, cause, date, or event as certain merely because it matches a symbol.
- Never use divination as the sole basis for medical, legal, financial, safety-critical, or irreversible decisions.
- Do not accuse anyone of theft, concealment, dishonesty, or harm without independent factual evidence.
- Prefer reversible, low-cost checks. Escalate urgent loss cases to ordinary measures such as device tracking, account security, police reports, property management, or document reissuance.
- Do not retrofit every new detail to the reading. Record predictions before checking reality.
- Do not combine incompatible systems silently. Identify each method and keep their results separate before comparison.

## Required inputs

Collect what is available; mark every missing item rather than inventing it.

1. **Exact question** — one concrete target and decision, such as “Which area should be searched first?”
2. **Target facts** — object, last confirmed time and place, usual storage habits, who had legitimate access, movements since last seen, and searches already completed.
3. **Candidate hypotheses** — named rooms, containers, people-controlled areas, or a bounded “elsewhere” option.
4. **Chosen tradition** — method, lineage or source convention if known, and whether the user supplied or requested a casting.
5. **Casting record** — original numbers, time, place/time zone, chart, hexagram, changing lines, or other raw inputs. Preserve them verbatim.
6. **Source material** — editions, translations, commentaries, links, scans, or quotations the user wants applied.
7. **Constraints** — permission to search, privacy boundaries, deadlines, replacement costs, and actions that must not be taken.

If no valid casting or source basis exists, offer a factual search analysis or explain what is required. Do not fabricate a chart, transmission rule, quotation, or classical authority.

## Maintain three independent tracks

### Track A — Traditional-source reasoning

Record the method and derive meanings only through disclosed rules.

- Distinguish primary/classical text, later commentary, modern practitioner convention, and the current interpreter’s synthesis.
- Cite exact sources when available. If attribution cannot be verified, label it “unverified convention” rather than presenting it as ancient doctrine.
- Show the mapping chain: raw symbol → documented traditional meaning → contextual interpretation → candidate hypothesis.
- Note alternative readings and school-dependent rules.
- Treat “one matter, one casting” and similar rules as method-specific conventions, not universal historical facts, unless supported by a cited source.

### Track B — Modern probabilistic reasoning

Build an ordinary-world hypothesis model independent of the divination result.

- Establish base rates from habits, custody, recency, visibility, prior searches, household document organization, and physical fit.
- Ask what evidence would be expected if each hypothesis were true.
- Rank hypotheses qualitatively (`high`, `medium`, `low`) or numerically only when defensible inputs exist.
- Do not convert symbolic correspondences into invented statistical probabilities.
- Identify confirmation bias, suggestion effects, hindsight bias, availability bias, and duplicated evidence.
- Include at least one mundane alternative and an “unknown/other” hypothesis.

### Track C — Factual status and uncertainty

Maintain a compact fact ledger:

| Item | Status | Basis | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Last confirmed observation | verified / reported / unknown | direct observation, message, memory | high / medium / low |
| Candidate location searched | complete / partial / not searched | search log | high / medium / low |
| Traditional association | sourced / conventional / speculative | citation or disclosed inference | high / medium / low |
| Location claim | hypothesis / ruled down / verified | current evidence | high / medium / low |

Reserve `verified` for observation or reliable records. A symbolic match is never factual verification.

## Workflow

### 1. Frame the question

Rewrite the request as a bounded research question. Separate:

- interpretive question: “What does this casting traditionally suggest?”
- factual question: “Where is the object actually located?”
- operational question: “What should be checked next?”

Answer each at its proper evidence level.

### 2. Freeze the initial record

Before interpreting, record the exact question, time, raw casting inputs, candidate locations, known facts, and prior search results. Do not alter this baseline after feedback arrives.

If the user proposes a new precise location after an earlier reading, treat it as a new hypothesis. Test whether it was independently predicted; do not call it a successful prediction merely because it can be symbolically accommodated.

### 3. Validate the method

Confirm that required inputs and calculation rules fit the selected tradition. Recompute only when rules are explicit. If multiple legitimate calculation conventions produce different charts, show the alternatives or ask the user to select a convention.

### 4. Derive the traditional reading

Create an auditable interpretation table:

| Raw feature | Source or convention | Traditional meaning | Contextual inference | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Keep direct source claims distinct from analogy. Avoid false precision such as an exact shelf, direction, distance, or time unless the method explicitly yields it and label it as an interpretive hypothesis even then.

### 5. Build the mundane baseline

Rank candidate hypotheses without using the casting. For loss-location analysis, consider:

- last confirmed custody and normal storage location;
- who moved, filed, cleaned, packed, or transported related items;
- object dimensions and plausible containers;
- search quality: visual scan versus item-by-item removal;
- document grouping, misfiling, nesting, and adjacency to related records;
- whether the candidate area is frequently disturbed or rarely accessed.

### 6. Compare without collapsing

Show agreement and disagreement between Tracks A and B. A location supported by both may receive higher **search priority**, but not higher factual certainty until checked. Explain which part of the ranking comes from tradition and which from mundane evidence.

### 7. Produce a falsifiable search plan

For each candidate, specify:

- exact bounded zone;
- inspection method;
- expected clues;
- stopping condition;
- time or effort cap;
- result to record.

Search one zone systematically before moving on. For documents, remove and inspect folders, envelopes, book interiors, rear gaps, and related-file groups when permission allows; do not rely on a glance.

### 8. Update from reality

After each check, append the observation and update only affected hypotheses.

- **Found:** record the actual location and compare it with the pre-registered claims. Count partial matches honestly.
- **Not found after complete search:** lower that hypothesis; do not create an ad hoc symbolic excuse.
- **Partial search:** preserve uncertainty and state what remains unchecked.
- **New factual evidence:** update the mundane model first, then note whether the traditional interpretation changes or becomes obsolete.
- **Repeated misses:** stop refining symbolic detail. Switch to inventory reconstruction, relevant contacts, tracking/security steps, or replacement procedures.

Never erase the original ranking. Keep a dated change log: observation → affected hypothesis → revised priority → reason.

## Evidence labels

Use these labels consistently:

- **Verified fact** — directly observed or supported by a reliable record.
- **Reported fact** — supplied by a person but not independently verified.
- **Traditional source claim** — traceable to a named text or commentary.
- **Practitioner convention** — method custom with uncertain or later provenance.
- **Interpretive inference** — the analyst’s contextual mapping.
- **Modern hypothesis** — ordinary probabilistic explanation.
- **Unknown** — insufficient evidence; do not fill the gap.

## Output format

Use this order:

1. **Scope note** — one sentence stating the cultural/research boundary.
2. **Question and known facts** — exact target, raw inputs, and missing data.
3. **Traditional reading** — method, sources, mapping table, alternatives, and limitations.
4. **Modern reasoning** — independent base-rate analysis and bias checks.
5. **Combined search priorities** — ranked candidates with separate rationales from Tracks A and B; include `unknown/other`.
6. **Reality-check plan** — bounded, permission-respecting checks with stopping rules.
7. **Confidence statement** — confidence in the interpretation and confidence in the factual conclusion stated separately.
8. **Update log** — include when prior searches or later feedback exist.

Use calibrated language such as “fits this traditional association,” “raises search priority,” “weakly supports,” and “remains unverified.” Avoid “the divination proves,” “definitely,” “destined,” or “it must be there.”

## Minimum quality check

Before responding, confirm that:

- the original question and casting data are preserved;
- no source, quotation, lineage, calculation, or probability was invented;
- traditional interpretation, modern reasoning, and facts are visibly distinct;
- at least one alternative hypothesis and one disconfirming test are present;
- all concrete claims carry an evidence label or clear basis;
- advice is reversible, lawful, privacy-respecting, and proportionate;
- the response states what would change the ranking;
- uncertainty remains explicit even when multiple clues align.
